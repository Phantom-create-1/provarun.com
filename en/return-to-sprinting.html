Training / Starting again

Sprinting as an adult: a four‑week return to running fast

Running fast again begins with controlled steps. A guide back to acceleration, rhythm and a time of your own.

By Paul Knutzen
23 November 2026

At a glance

Begin at a controlled pace, increase the load gradually and allow enough recovery between fast runs. Step by step, you can rediscover acceleration, rhythm and a time of your own. Your response to the training matters more than the calendar.

Perhaps you run regularly but have not sprinted for years. Perhaps your mind remembers the pace of school athletics while your body has settled into different habits. You do not need a target time to begin again. You need somewhere with room to accelerate and slow down, and a willingness to start from where you are today.

Build around gradual loading and recovery

The load should fit your current experience. WHO recommends beginning physical activity in small amounts and gradually increasing frequency, intensity and duration. That is a general movement principle, not an instruction to sprint flat out immediately. These four weeks are an editorial guide based on general physical-activity and sprint-training principles. [WHO, 2020]

Haugen and colleagues also describe gradual adaptation during the opening weeks of sprint training. Their review mainly concerns elite sprinters and coaching practice. The principles of progression, individual adjustment and recovery are useful here; elite training volumes are not being presented as a beginner schedule. A controlled run is a worthwhile beginning even if it feels unlike the sprint you remember. [Haugen et al., 2019]

Seek medical advice before returning if you have health concerns, and stop training if you experience pain.

Week 1: rediscover the movement

The first week is about a calm, repeatable routine. Find a clear, level stretch with room to run through and slow down. Warm up with easy movement before moving into controlled faster runs. Your aim is to notice acceleration and rhythm again. You do not have to find your maximum speed or check an old personal best.

Build pace gently within the run instead of putting everything into the first step. Notice whether you can keep moving freely and slow down under control afterwards. Between runs, take enough time to feel ready again. Afterwards, record how the movement felt and how you respond later. That observation matters more than the stopwatch at this stage.

The calendar week is not a test you must pass. If you have not found a suitable place, or the movement still feels unfamiliar, repeat this introduction. The next stage can wait. This guide is meant to make decisions easier, rather than create another deadline in your week.

Week 2: favour quality over extra load

During the second week, aim for a more familiar routine. Keep the location and warm-up as consistent as possible. If the previous runs have felt manageable and you have recovered well, you can make some runs a little quicker. Avoid increasing speed, total running and training frequency together. That leaves you better able to recognise which change you are responding to.

Allow full recovery between fast repetitions. In Haugen and colleagues' sprint review, this helps preserve the quality of the next effort. The authors also recommend ending a session when performance or technique deteriorates. It is a useful principle for returning adults: a visibly worsening run is not a reason to add another one. [Haugen et al., 2019]

After the session, ask whether the last repetition was as controlled as the first. If you were mainly trying to get through it, stay with the previous step. In this guide, a repeatable movement is a sign of progress; accumulating extra fatigue is not a goal in itself.

Week 3: record a baseline time

An initial time becomes useful when you can repeat its setup. Choose a distance you have already run with control. Decide on the start procedure and use a timing method you can use again for the next comparison. This run is there to establish a baseline. It does not need to beat a performance from years ago.

Record the distance, start procedure and any obvious conditions alongside the time. Timing from a starting signal is different from timing only from first movement. Haugen and Buchheit show how strongly start procedures and measurement setups can affect results. A small change in the number is therefore not automatically a change in your physical performance. [Haugen & Buchheit, 2016]

If you use phone video, review the selected start and finish moments afterwards. If one is unclear, mark the result as uncertain in your own notes. The guide to timing sprints with your phone explains this in more detail. You are collecting a reference point, rather than passing judgement on your body.

Week 4: choose a routine you can sustain

By the end of this guide, aim to have a routine you want to continue. Repeat the familiar setup and assess how you are handling the load. If you record another time, keep the start and measurement method the same. Compare more than the number: consider how controlled the run felt and whether you had enough recovery between efforts.

You can stay with the same amount of training afterwards. You can repeat a session or take a step back. Increasing the load is an option, rather than an obligation when the week changes. The fourth week is not a promised deadline for maximal sprinting. It is a useful point to make an honest decision about the weeks ahead.

If you want a sporting reference, the guide to 100-m and 400-m times explains what official lists can tell you. The records listed there are not beginner targets. Your own series of comparable runs remains the most immediate, practical benchmark.

Give sprinting a place in your wider training

Sprinting does not have to carry your whole week's physical activity. WHO's general adult recommendations include 150–300 minutes of moderate or 75–150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity weekly, plus muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days. These figures are not a recommendation to fill the vigorous minutes with maximal sprints. Easy movement and other forms of training still have their place. [WHO, 2020]

For what makes a fast run distinctive, read Why humans sprint: force transfer, muscle work and the demands of short ground contacts. At the beginning, a simpler question is enough: can you repeat the movement with control and recover from it? If you can, you have a more useful starting point than a single run against a memory.

Frequently asked questions

How should I use the four weeks?

Work through the steps at your own pace. Repeat a week if the movement still feels unfamiliar, and only progress once you are coping well with the runs so far.

Do I have to run faster every week?

No. Stay with the previous step if the load still feels unfamiliar or recovery is inadequate. A new calendar week is not a reason to increase automatically.

When is it useful to record a first time?

When you can run a familiar distance with control and repeat the start procedure and timing method. Use the result as a baseline for later comparable runs.
