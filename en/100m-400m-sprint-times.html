How fast is fast? Understanding 100‑m and 400‑m times

A time needs a reference point. Official records and performance lists can help, once you know what they represent.

By Paul Knutzen
23 November 2026

At a glance

The official sources checked here do not provide representative average 100-m or 400-m times for adult recreational runners. World and masters records describe exceptional performance limits. To track your progress, begin with your own runs using the same start procedure and timing method.

You cross the line, look at the time and wonder: was that fast? It is a reasonable question, but a number alone says very little. A fair comparison needs the distance, a relevant reference group and the measurement conditions. An electronically timed race and a hand-timed training run can produce similar-looking numbers while describing different things.

There is no supported recreational average here

The official sources checked for this article do not provide a representative average for adult recreational runners. World Athletics publishes records and performance lists. The German athletics federation, DLV, publishes selected annual lists, including the top 50 for 2025. These document competition performances by a selected group of athletes, rather than a random sample of adults. [DLV: annual lists]

That is why this guide has no invented thresholds labelled “beginner”, “good” or “excellent”. Those categories would need a clearly described dataset: who was measured, how old they were, and how the start and finish were timed. Without that information, a classification looks more precise than it is. You can choose a personal target without turning it into a universal norm.

World records describe the very top

Official world records provide clear reference points for elite performance. World Athletics lists Usain Bolt's 9.58 s for the men's 100 m and Wayde van Niekerk's 43.03 s for the men's 400 m. The women's records are Florence Griffith-Joyner's 10.49 s and Marita Koch's 47.60 s. These entries were checked on 23 November 2026. [World Athletics: 100 m] [World Athletics: 400 m]

These are not entry-level targets. They describe the best recognised performances in those categories. They show where the sporting peak lies. They do not tell you how fast someone should run after a working day, without sprint training or after a long break. The gap between you and a world record is therefore not a useful verdict on the value of your training.

Age groups offer another perspective

Masters records show that exceptional sprinting remains possible later in adulthood. World Masters Athletics uses five-year age groups. M50 and W50 here cover men and women aged 50–54. The figures below are outdoor world records, rather than averages or championship records. The record lists used are dated 1 November 2026. [WMA: age groups]

The men's figures come from WMA's official men's list, and the women's figures from its women's list. They document exceptional performances within each age group. They do not show what an average person of that age runs or ought to achieve. Putting a record in an age group does not turn it into a training prescription. [WMA: men] [WMA: women]

A performance list describes its participants

Performance lists help when you want to understand documented competition results. Begin with the heading: year, age category, sex, event, and whether the list covers indoor or outdoor competition. Then check which performances qualify for inclusion. DLV's publication of its 2025 annual lists explicitly describes top-50 lists. Being absent does not automatically make someone slow; the list has a different purpose. [DLV]

If you are considering your first competition, results from your local setting may be more informative than the world record. They still represent people who took part in the recorded events. Read them as a description of that field. They cannot establish what everyone of your age or with your training history can do.

Match the start and timing conditions

Comparable times need the same definition of beginning and end. World Athletics rules specify that fully automatic timing is started automatically by the starting signal. The finish is judged by the torso, rather than the head, arms or feet. For the relevant 100-m records, an assisting wind must not exceed +2.0 m/s. The 400 m has no equivalent wind limit for record recognition. [World Athletics: rules]

A training measurement beginning with first movement starts at a different event from a race time beginning with the signal. Wind and the measurement setup also affect comparisons. Haugen and Buchheit explain why those differences matter when assessing performance. Before celebrating a small improvement or worrying about a slower result, check the conditions. [Haugen & Buchheit, 2016]

If you use your phone, the guide to video timing accuracy explains frame rate, start procedures and repeatable camera placement. Video is useful because the selected moments can be reviewed. That does not automatically make the result an officially recognised competition time.

Your most useful comparison starts with you

A well-documented baseline run is particularly valuable for tracking your own progress. Choose a distance and a start procedure. Use the same measurement method next time. Note obvious changes and keep several results. You will build a personal series whose origins you understand. It answers your training question better than an unsupported table found online.

Compare each distance with itself first. A 100-m time does not imply a fixed 400-m time. The longer run is a different task; simply multiplying by four is only arithmetic. For what sprinting asks of your body, read Why humans sprint. If you are returning after a break, begin with the return-to-sprinting guide and a present-day time as your reference.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good 100-m time for an adult?

This guide has no supported universal threshold. The official sources checked provide competition lists and records, not representative average times for adult recreational runners.

What do M50 and W50 mean?

In the WMA age groups used here, M50 covers men and W50 covers women aged 50–54.

Can a 100-m time predict my 400-m time?

The sources used here do not establish a fixed conversion. First compare each distance with your own runs over that same distance.
