Timing a sprint with your phone: how accurate is it?

Video lets you check how a time was measured. Its accuracy depends on the frame rate and the start and finish you choose.

By Paul Knutzen
23 November 2026

At a glance

At a constant frame rate, adjacent video frames are 1/fps seconds apart: about 33 ms at 30 fps and 17 ms at 60 fps. That interval is not a guarantee of measurement accuracy. Comparable sprint times also require a consistent start procedure, finish criterion and camera setup.

A sprint time can have two decimal places and still be inaccurate. The display tells you how a number is presented. The measurement depends on when timing starts, when it ends and what information is available between those events. Phone video offers a practical advantage: you can return to the chosen moments and inspect them again.

Frame rate sets the time grid

More frames per second mean smaller gaps in time. At a constant frame rate of f frames per second, adjacent frames are Δt = 1/f seconds apart. Multiply by 1000 to express the result in milliseconds. The table shows rounded values. [Calculation]

“fps” means frames per second. These figures describe the video's time grid. They do not mean every result is accurate to within that interval. You still have to choose the right frame. If the runner is blurred or the line is hidden, the choice remains uncertain. A finer grid cannot recover visual information the recording never captured.

With a variable frame rate, adjacent frames are not necessarily equally spaced. Their actual timing information matters; an average fps value is not a universal calculation rule. Provarun distinguishes detected and variable frame rates in its review interface, and warns that low frame rates make start and finish selection coarser. [Provarun: video review]

Frame interval is not total measurement error

A small interval describes temporal resolution, not overall accuracy. Choose the wrong start frame and even a perfectly clear finish frame will not fix the result. The reverse is also true. Start and finish are separate choices that both contribute to the elapsed time. “60 fps” alone therefore cannot support a blanket accuracy promise.

Frame-by-frame review does not automatically make a system official competition timing. It makes the events you selected inspectable. That can be useful in training: you can spot a doubtful choice and revisit it. Establishing how accurately a particular system measures against a reference would require a separate validation study.

Hand timing has no reliable universal conversion

A handheld stopwatch is not interchangeable with electronic timing. Hetzler and colleagues compared the methods during a 200-m sprint, recording splits at 25-m intervals. Hand times were sometimes faster and sometimes slower than electronic times. A small average difference concealed larger errors in individual measurements. [Hetzler et al., 2008]

The distribution of those errors prevented a reliable general correction factor. For your own records, that means adding a fixed amount to a hand-timed school result does not make it equivalent to an electronically timed performance. Keep the original timing method attached to the number. This study tested handheld stopwatches, not a current version of Provarun. [Hetzler et al., 2008]

The start is part of the result

Timing from a signal measures a different interval from timing from first movement. In the former, the reaction between the signal and moving is included. In the latter, timing begins only when movement starts. A run-up before the measured section changes the task again. A flying start and a standing start therefore need separate descriptions in your training record.

Haugen and Buchheit's methodological review shows how strongly different starts and triggering systems can alter sprint times. The differences can exceed performance changes achieved over years of training. Wind and other external conditions also affect the result. A new personal best only becomes meaningful when its setup is known. [Haugen & Buchheit, 2016]

A useful setup is repeatable

Choose an arrangement you can reproduce next time. Place your iPhone securely beside the track, on the extended finish line. The line and the runner crossing it should be visible. Check the framing before running. An oblique view or an obscured line makes the finish moment harder to judge. [Provarun: setup]

Decide beforehand which visible event will count as the finish, then use it consistently. After the run, check that you really applied the same criterion. Imported footage also needs an identifiable start event. A clip that begins after you have started moving cannot supply the complete time for a standing-start run.

Record at least the distance, start procedure and timing method alongside each result. Add obvious changes such as a headwind, different shoes or a different surface. You do not need to turn training into a laboratory. The note is there to help you read two performances fairly later. The main sprinting article explains why these conditions matter.

Look for a pattern over time

Several comparable runs tell you more than an isolated outlier. Haugen and Buchheit recommend considering measurement error alongside actual performance change. Repeated observations can improve the assessment. In practice, save ordinary runs too, and inspect an unexpected jump in the video before treating it as progress. [Haugen & Buchheit, 2016]

For context around your result, the guide to 100-m and 400-m times offers official reference points and explains their limits. If these are your first runs after a break, start with the return-to-sprinting guide. A well-documented time today is a more useful starting point than a polished memory of a run years ago.

Frequently asked questions

Does 60 fps mean accuracy to 0.01 seconds?

No. At a constant 60 fps, adjacent frames are about 16.67 milliseconds apart. Overall measurement accuracy also depends on start and finish selection and the setup.

Can I convert a hand time to an electronic time?

Hetzler and colleagues could not derive a reliable general correction factor. Compare results recorded with the same timing method.

Why does the start matter so much?

Timing from a signal includes the reaction to it; timing from first movement begins later. These procedures measure different intervals.
